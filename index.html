Auto: plug-in share of new vehicles

How much of each car maker's output will be plug-in vehicles by 2030, built mainly from what the makers themselves publish.

What the dataset is

The dataset holds one row per car maker per year, for 41 vehicle makers worldwide that together reported 90.3 million vehicles in 2025. Each row gives the maker's total volume and the share of it that is plug-in: battery electric, plug-in hybrid or fuel cell. Ordinary hybrids, which cannot be charged from the grid, do not count.

Weighted by volume, the plug-in share across these makers rises from 24% in 2025 to 45% in 2030. This is one best estimate, with no policy scenarios of our own: each maker moves towards the best published evidence of where it is heading. 28% of 2030 volume rests on outside forecasts, and the only projection made here is the 2030 volume that turns targets stated in vehicles (Toyota and Kia) into shares. Kia states its own total alongside, and that total is used.

Against an outside view

The International Energy Agency puts the world plug-in share at 42% in 2030 under stated policies. The dataset's 45% is higher, and so is the bottom of its range. The figures are not like for like: the agency describes the whole market under current policy, while the dataset describes what this set of makers say they intend where they say it, and outside forecasts elsewhere, the agency's own among them.

What it is not

The figure is an average across the makers that could be sourced, not a share of the world market. A maker that publishes nothing usable is absent, and the volumes are those the makers report, on the basis each one chooses. Added up, they come to 110% of the world car market as the International Energy Agency sizes it. 3.1 million vehicles built by 5 Chinese joint ventures are reported by both partners, and 1.4 million are vans and trucks inside group totals; without both the set stands at 104%. Mahindra, Tata Motors and Mitsubishi report fiscal years.

A maker to follow

This chapter follows Volkswagen Group through each step. It reported 9.0 million vehicles in 2025, of which 16% were plug-in, and the dataset puts it at 50% in 2030. The next page shows where figures like these come from.

Sources

The dataset rests on 1,157 figures taken from 119 documents, published by the makers except where noted: annual reports, quarterly results, sales bulletins and investor presentations. 61 of the figures come from the outside sources below, and 5 from press reports. No press figure enters a starting share, but the targets of GAC, Dongfeng Passenger Vehicle and Mahindra, 5% of 2030 volume, are known only from the press. The figures also cover brands and truck units beyond the 41 makers. Each figure is stored with the document, the page and the exact words it was read from, so any number in the dataset can be checked against its source. 1,108 of the figures also carry a link to the document online; 292 of those links are reconstructed and not confirmed.

Outside sources are used to set a maker's 2030 share where it has published no target, to set the range of every maker, to scale volumes, and for the starting share of Suzuki, Nissan and Mazda. They are:

  • The International Energy Agency's Global EV Outlook 2025, for the plug-in share of each market under policies already stated, and for the size of the world car market.
  • InfluenceMap, a think tank that publishes a 2030 forecast of the electric share of production for 15 of the makers. It is built on 2023 production and counts vehicles built, not sold, so it predates the changes of plan several makers have announced since.

Read directly, or derived

829 figures are read directly from a document. The other 328 are derived from published figures by arithmetic, and each one stores its formula. The derivations are simple. The largest group, 141 figures, only converts units, for example a table printed in thousands. No figure is estimated by us; forecasts by the outside sources are stored as read.

For Volkswagen Group the dataset holds 23 figures, 18 of them read directly. One of the derived ones is recorded as “Any deliveries, Europe, 2025”. The company prints it by region, so the figure is computed as Western Europe 3,381,100 + Central and Eastern Europe 557,900.

A check against an independent collection

The same makers were first collected by hand in a spreadsheet. Where both hold the same figure, 448 agree within 0.5%, 3 within 2% and 22 differ; each difference was traced to the document, though which side was wrong was not recorded. No difference touches a figure the 2030 result uses; the starting share of GM rests on a figure that differs. Makers whose totals were checked this way carry 44% of 2030 volume. A further 684 figures in the dataset were never in the spreadsheet. With the figures in place, the next page builds each maker's starting point.

The starting point: 2025

A maker's plug-in share in 2025 is its plug-in vehicles divided by all its vehicles in that year, both taken from its own reports where it publishes them. Nothing is adjusted: if a maker publishes its plug-in volume for some regions only, the regions it leaves out count as zero. For Suzuki, Nissan and Mazda the share is InfluenceMap's, for an earlier year. 4 makers have no 2025 share in the dataset (Stellantis, Leapmotor, Li Auto and Aston Martin); they are left out of that year's average, and their paths start from an earlier share or from zero.

What counts as volume

Makers do not report on a common basis. 35 of the 41 are recorded as sales, which includes the deliveries of makers that sell direct. The other 6 publish deliveries, wholesale or production instead, and the dataset uses what each one publishes. The largest maker, Toyota, accounts for 12% of the 2025 volume.

Makers that start at the top

11 makers already sell only plug-in vehicles, 2 of them with no 2025 share recorded. They stay there.

Starting points known to be low

For Stellantis, Ford and GM the published plug-in volume is incomplete, for example covering one region or one business segment, while the total volume is global. Their 2025 shares are therefore too low. Together they make up 15% of volume. Where such a maker has a 2025 value, it pulls the 24% starting point of the whole set down. For Stellantis and GM it does not lower the 2030 result, because their 2030 shares come from forward evidence and not from the starting point. For Ford, 5% of volume, it does: its stated plan is converted with the same incomplete mix, so its 2030 value is a floor. The next page explains that forward evidence.

Looking forward

Each maker's 2030 share comes from the best evidence that exists for it. The kinds of evidence are ranked, and a maker uses the first kind it has:

  • Its own target, or already all plug-in. 24 makers, 58% of 2030 volume: 11 already all plug-in and 13 with a target. 7 of the targets, 34% of volume, are for battery cars alone, used as stated: the plug-in hybrids those makers sell today are left out. Holding them at their 2025 share would put the result at 46%. 3 targets (Toyota, Volkswagen and GAC), 24% of volume, were stated before 2024, and Volkswagen and GAC have not repeated theirs since.
  • A company statement that is not a plug-in target as it stands. 4 makers, 14%: 3 targets that include ordinary hybrids, with the hybrids removed using the maker's current mix, and a stated expectation (Honda).
  • InfluenceMap's forecast for the maker. 3 makers, 7%. The forecast is not used for a maker that has withdrawn or cut back its plans since it was made: Stellantis and GM take the next kind instead. Keeping them on the forecast would give 47%.
  • The International Energy Agency's outlook for the markets the maker sells in. 10 makers, 21%. The outlook is weighted by the maker's own regional volumes where it publishes them (20 makers); 16 take the outlook of their home market whole, and 5 that publish no split take the world average.
  • None of these. No maker is left without evidence. PACCAR, a truck maker, was taken out of the set: no count of its zero-emission trucks and no target exists in its own documents.

A straight line to the evidence

The share moves in a straight line from the 2025 value to the evidence value for 2030, through any published interim target (Mercedes-Benz and Mahindra). A target for an earlier year is held level from then on: the value for Mercedes-Benz Group blends Mercedes-Benz Cars at 40% with Mercedes-Benz Vans at its 2026 target of 20%. No maker is ever projected below its own 2025 share. Nothing is fitted to past growth.

Volkswagen Group is on the strongest kind of evidence. The dataset records its target as: 50% BEV globally by 2030 — NEW AUTO strategy release 13 Jul 2021. It is a target for battery cars alone, stated in 2021, and the company has neither restated nor withdrawn it. The dataset therefore takes it from 16% to 50% in a straight line.

Volumes

Every maker keeps its 2025 share of the world car market, so its volume grows at the market's rate in the International Energy Agency's stated-policies outlook, +15.9% by 2030. The agency prints shares in whole percents, so that rate could lie anywhere from +12.7% to +19.2%; it matters only through the targets stated in vehicles, and across that span the result stays between 45.2% and 45.4%. Together the makers reach 104.6 million vehicles in 2030. The combined share in every year weights each maker by this 2030 volume, so the line shows only how their shares change.

How far the result could move

The range is not a statistical interval. It is the same calculation run again with every maker at the lowest, and then the highest, of the evidence values that exist for it: its own target, its converted statement, InfluenceMap's forecast and the International Energy Agency's outlook. That gives 44% to 55% around the 45% result.

No value is taken below a maker's starting share. For 22 makers the evidence values then differ; for the other 19 they do not, and there is no range. Most of the makers with a range, 15, already sit at their lowest value, which is why the range reaches further up than down.

The range takes the evidence as it stands. Dropping InfluenceMap's forecasts, so that the 3 makers on them take the agency's outlook, gives 45%. Replacing the targets not repeated since 2024 with each maker's next evidence gives 46%, higher than the result, because that evidence is the market outlook for China and a forecast that counts plug-in hybrids. Continuing those makers' own change over the last year instead gives 44%, and doing the same for all 23 makers with a year-on-year change of their own, 72% of volume, gives 40%, below the range.

Limits

  • Mixed volume bases. Sales, deliveries, wholesale and production are added together as reported.
  • Joint ventures. Output of Chinese joint ventures can appear under both partners; no single ownership convention is applied.
  • Incomplete starting points. Stellantis, Ford and GM start too low, as described earlier.
  • Battery-only targets. Plug-in hybrids are left out for Toyota, Volkswagen, Suzuki, Kia, BMW, Mazda and Mahindra.
  • Targets are intentions. A published target is taken at its word until the maker withdraws it. The maker then moves to the next kind of evidence, which may itself predate the withdrawal.

Air: CO₂ from passenger flights

How much CO₂ each airline's flights emit today and by 2031, estimated for every airline, aircraft and route at the same time.

What the dataset is

The dataset holds one row per aircraft per year: 31,158 aircraft in service in 2026, and 41,412 once those arriving later are counted. Each row gives the airline group that flies the aircraft, its type, the distance it flies, the passengers it carries, the fuel it burns and the CO₂ this produces. These are worked out per airline and aircraft type and shared equally among that airline's aircraft of the type. 55 cargo and off-network operators, with 577 aircraft, are left out, and freighters inside passenger airlines are not counted. The aircraft belong to 697 airline groups and fly 48,625 one-way routes, 24,332 airport pairs, between 3,560 airports.

Together they emit 835 million tonnes of CO₂ in 2026 and 981 in 2031. Passengers grow from 4.9 to 6.0 billion, and CO₂ per passenger flight falls from 171 to 164 kilograms. This is the model's best fit, with no policy scenarios.

Why it has to be estimated

No source reports emissions by airline for the whole world. Departures boards say where airlines fly. Registers say which aircraft they own. Some regulators count flights or passengers, and the European trading scheme verifies CO₂ for flights in its area. Each covers a part. The dataset is the single set of values that agrees best with all of them at once.

What the 2026 figures rest on

Measured by CO₂, 81% comes from airline fleets with direct evidence on their size or hours, and 18% from flying inferred from similar airlines. The remaining 1% belongs to fleets larger than any source supports, or that registers list but tracking never saw. 30% of the aircraft are placeholders: the model's count for an airline and type exceeds the aircraft the registers name. All CO₂ is assigned to passengers, none to cargo in the hold.

An airline to follow

This chapter follows Vueling. In 2026 the dataset gives it 144 aircraft of 5 types, 35.3 million passengers and 3.1 million tonnes of CO₂, which is 87 kilograms per passenger flight. The next page shows what such figures rest on.

Sources

The model draws on 24 groups of sources, some of them used only for checking. The main ones are:

  • Routes. Airport departures boards, which we collect daily, give the passenger routes flown in the weeks observed and how often. Those weeks are scaled to a year with the monthly traffic pattern that regulators publish for 880 airports in Europe, the United States and Brazil, and with a regional pattern elsewhere. That scaling is itself adjusted to fit the rest of the evidence.
  • Counted flights. Aviation authorities in the United States, Brazil, Colombia, the United Kingdom and Japan publish departures by airline and route. Many more publish totals by airport.
  • Aircraft tracking. Public records of aircraft positions show which airline flew a route and set a lower limit on each airline's fleet and flying hours.
  • Fleets. National registers, the manufacturers' own counts and their order books.
  • Fuel. Fuel burned per kilometre by aircraft type, from the filings of American airlines and from EUROCONTROL, the European air traffic agency. Elsewhere the European level stands; at the American level 2026 CO₂ would be +8.1% higher.
  • Verified CO₂. The emissions that airlines report, and auditors verify, under the European trading scheme. The British scheme is used only as a check.
  • Growth. The market forecasts that Airbus, Boeing and COMAC publish.

What the sources pin down

From these sources come 49 kinds of evidence, 156,529 individual figures in all. 84,482 are read from sources; the rest are rules and physical limits. They bear on different parts of the calculation:

Coverage is uneven. Regulators count the flights on 28% of routes. On every other route the number of flights starts from the departures boards and is adjusted to fit the rest of the evidence.

A regulator counts Vueling's flights only on its routes to the United Kingdom. What holds its other figures in place is the departures boards, the tracking of its aircraft, its fleet and the CO₂ it has verified under the European scheme. In the dataset every aircraft type it flies has direct evidence on its fleet or its hours.

Data methodologyAir

Everything is estimated at once

CO₂ is the end of a chain. Flights on a route are shared between the airlines that fly it. Each airline's flights are flown by aircraft of particular types. The types and distances give the hours flown, the fuel burned and the seats offered.

If the links were estimated one after another, evidence used late would overwrite what was settled early, and verified CO₂ could never correct a wrong number of flights. So the model looks for the values of all links together. Each piece of evidence enters once, with its own stated tolerance, and the model finds the values that miss the evidence by the least overall. There are 219,653 unknowns. Where no evidence reaches one, it stays at a plain default, such as the departures boards being right.

How closely the evidence is met

33 kinds of evidence are met within their tolerance and 11 are strained. 1 is missed outright: the count of each aircraft type worldwide, which should change from year to year only by deliveries and retirements. Its retirements fall short of what its own survival curves expect. These verdicts are for a kind of evidence as a whole; single figures within a kind can miss by much more. The other 4 record that a regulator lists no flights by an airline on a route, and the model sets those to zero. The hardest evidence to satisfy is on how many aircraft an airline has and on the mix of aircraft types, where what a source covers has to be matched to the model's own routes.

Looking forward

Every year to 2031 is part of the same estimate as 2026. A later year differs from it in these ways.

  • Demand. Each route grows at the rate the manufacturers forecast for the regions it connects. Forecasts exist for 98% of routes; the typical route grows 3.4% a year. Passengers overall grow faster, by 4.2% a year, because routes in fast-growing markets grow much faster than the typical one.
  • Aircraft leaving. Existing aircraft retire according to their type and age: 945 by 2031, 0.6% of the fleet a year. A further 2,191 move to another airline. Build years come from registers and delivery records; 24% are filled in from the type.
  • Aircraft arriving. The order books have 7,721 deliveries due from 2027, and 95% of them are placed. 47% cannot be tied to an airline already flying that type, and the model places them with the airlines that need them. Where an airline still needs more aircraft than deliveries and transfers give, the model adds them: 740 by 2031, backed neither by an order nor by another airline giving them up.
  • What stays the same. The share of seats filled and the fuel each type burns per kilometre do not change. There is one CO₂ factor per kilogram of fuel in every year, so no cleaner fuels. Emissions per passenger improve only because the mix of aircraft types and routes shifts.

The fleet grows from 31,158 to 38,276 aircraft. CO₂ grows 3.3% a year, more slowly than passengers, and CO₂ per passenger flight changes by −4.2% over the period.

For Vueling the fleet goes from 144 to 167 aircraft and CO₂ from 3.1 to 3.5 million tonnes.

How far the result could move

The model is run 240 times with its fitted values varied within what the evidence allows. The result is the best fit itself; the runs give the range around it, which between the lowest and the highest tenth is 977 to 984 million tonnes in 2031. That is 0.7% of the total, and it is too narrow to be read as the real uncertainty: it varies the fitted values only, and takes each route's flights and airline split, the fleet changes after 2026, the order books and the growth forecasts as given. Figures for single aircraft add up to the airline and world totals.

For a single aircraft the range, measured a little more widely, is far larger: 27% of its own CO₂ in 2026 and 57% in 2031. The fit itself accounts for most of it. Disagreement between the growth forecasts and the timing of deliveries add little.

Against what airlines report

For 33 airline groups the model's CO₂ inside the European and British trading schemes was set against what they verified there. The middle group stands at 102% of its verified figure and the bulk of the groups lie between 76% and 115%; 61% are within 15%. Vueling is at 104%. Against the worldwide CO₂ that 19 groups publish for themselves, cargo flights included, the middle is 90%. This spread, not the narrow range above, is the error to expect for one airline.

A test on airlines kept out

The passengers filed by 16 airlines in 3 regions were kept out of the estimate and compared with it afterwards. A range meant to contain the true figure 80% of the time contains it for 4 of the 16, so the ranges are too narrow, mainly because an airline is not always given all of its own flights.

A second test removes whole routes. On 17,792 such routes the model's split of flights between airlines is wrong by 18%, against 24% for a simple rule based on airline size. The number of flights on an unseen route is not predicted well: where no count exists, that number rests on the departures boards.

Limits

  • Retirements are understated. The model retires 189 aircraft a year, 0.6% of the fleet, on survival curves fitted to years when retirements were unusually low. The fleet therefore grows +22.8%, faster than public fleet forecasts, and ages more than in reality, which understates the gain in efficiency.
  • Fleets no source supports carry at least 1% of 2026 CO₂, and the 740 aircraft the model adds have no order behind them.
  • Verified CO₂ is met only loosely, because a trading scheme's area has to be rebuilt from route endpoints.
  • The level of fuel burn is a choice outside Europe and North America, where neither level is measured.
  • Seats filled are held constant, since the available series are level or falling a little.

Oil: crude output, field by field

What each oil field produces today and is likely to produce by 2030, built up from individual fields and tied to each country's reported total.

What the dataset is

The dataset holds one row per field or project per year. 13,482 fields and projects produce in 2025, in 89 countries. It covers crude oil and lease condensate. Biofuels and natural gas liquids are outside it, so its totals are lower than figures quoted for all liquids.

Output is 84.6 million barrels a day in 2025 and 85.5 in 2030, a change of +1.1%. This is one best estimate, with no price or policy scenarios. It assumes OPEC+ members stay at their quota, a choice sized near the end of the chapter.

Why a steady total hides large movements

Every barrel is placed in one of the 4 layers of the chart, so the same fields can be read in 3 ways.

  • With new additions is the total above: everything that produces today plus the projects already decided.
  • As it is now leaves the new additions out and changes nothing else. Drilling continues at its recent pace on shale and oil sands plays and in countries, chiefly Russia and China, whose mature fields are kept up by steady drilling. Output then falls to 80.3 million barrels a day, a change of −5.1%.
  • No new investment stops all spending at the start of 2026, drilling included. Output falls to 55.7, a change of −34.1%.

Sources

  • The field list is the Global Energy Monitor, Global Oil and Gas Extraction Tracker, March 2026 (CC BY 4.0). It names each field, its location, its status and, for many, a production figure for some recent year.
  • Measured output comes from regulators that publish production field by field, such as those of Norway, Brazil, the United Kingdom, the United States and the Canadian provinces.
  • National totals are the international statistics of the United States Energy Information Administration. Countries such as Norway and Brazil use their regulator's own total, so the dataset stands at 100.2% of the world total on that basis.
  • Decline rates, plateau lengths and new projects come from published studies and from the International Energy Agency.
  • Who operates and owns each field comes from the field list, from regulators' own operator and lease records, and from company annual reports and saved pages that name the company for the field.

Across oil and gas together the model draws on 824 sources and 1,843 recorded assumptions. Each is stored with its source, or with the reason for the choice.

Where each country's 2025 figure comes from

Of the 2025 total, 36% comes from production records by field, block or lease, published by regulators and in places by companies, and 25% rests on the field list's own figure. A further 24% comes from company and agency figures, used where neither exists; the largest case is Saudi Arabia, sized from the company's own prospectus. The remaining 15% is not attached to any named field. The next page explains it.

The mix differs sharply between countries. The United States takes 90% of its output from regulator series, while in Russia 37% of output cannot be tied to a named field.

The following pages follow one field, Johan Sverdrup in Norway, which produced 634 thousand barrels a day in 2025. It is a measured field: its figure is the Norwegian regulator's own monthly series, not an estimate.

From fields to a national total

Each field's 2025 output is taken from the best figure available for it. A regulator's measurement replaces the field list's figure for the same barrels. Where the field list's figure is for an earlier year, it is carried forward to 2025 along the path of the field's type, described on the next page.

The national remainder

The named fields of a country rarely add up to the output the country reports. The difference is kept as extra rows for the country, the national remainder, so that every country matches its reported total in 2025. It is a balancing figure, not a field.

73 countries carry a remainder. Together the remainders are 12.3 million barrels a day, 15% of the 2025 total; named fields and projects account for the other 85%. A remainder has no reservoir of its own, so it is given no decline curve. It follows the country's own trend over 2022 to 2025, and a rising trend is held level where new named fields explain the rise.

Field types

Named fields are given one of 7 types, from location, water depth, size and the kind of oil; company-level entries in Russia and China, 6% of output, have none. The type decides how long a field stays at its peak and how fast it declines afterwards, so it matters more for the forward view than for the starting point.

The types differ greatly in number: 7,614 small onshore fields and 4,575 shale oil units, many of them single leases, against only 138 giant onshore fields.

Who stands behind the output

A company is written on a field only where a saved source names it for that field, and a share only where the source prints it. On that rule 66% of 2025 output carries ownership shares and a further 19% names the operator alone. Within that, for 12% of output a regulator's records split the field between its operators by what each produced. The remainders have no field and so no company here. Of the 158 rows for new projects and expansions, 152 name a company and 137 carry shares, which covers 97% of their 2030 output.

What counts as a new addition

New additions are output that did not exist in 2025: projects decided, announced or under construction, expansions of producing fields, and fields still building up to their peak. Only what a project produces above its own 2025 level is an addition, so the additions start at nothing. Of the 158 rows, 123 carry an addition, 11 are netted inside OPEC+ members at their cap and 24 stay at or below their 2025 level. 103 had not started in 2025; the other 20 already produced 1.4 million barrels a day between them, which stays with the fields producing today.

Johan Sverdrup is of the type "Offshore shelf".

Looking forward

A producing field follows the path of its type: it holds its peak for as long as fields of that type have done in the past, then declines at the rate published for that type. A long enough regulator series gives a field its own measured decline instead. Shale, tight oil and oil sands do not decline in this way: new wells set their pace, measured over 2022 to 2025 (or a source's latest 4 years) and held to 2030.

Deepwater fields fall furthest, by 22.4%. Johan Sverdrup peaked in 2024. The regulator's latest months are below that peak, which ends its plateau here whatever its type would allow, so it declines from 2026 by 9.0% a year, from 634 to 396 thousand barrels a day.

New additions, OPEC+ and one adjusted year

  • New additions reach 5.2 million barrels a day by 2030. 26 projects that have started build up to their stated capacity, 2.7 million barrels a day in 2030, of which 1.4 was already flowing in 2025. The 69 not yet started begin 1 year later than announced and reach 80% of capacity. Brazil gives 29% of them, the 8 largest of 26 countries 81%.
  • OPEC+. The 12 members whose output is set by quota are capped at their average output over the last 3 months of 2025; where capacity falls below that, output falls with it. The members produce 38% of 2030 output, and 4 of them, with 22%, are still at the cap then. While it binds, a new project displaces the member's other fields, so 11 projects add nothing. The cap no longer covers the United Arab Emirates, which left the group in 2026: it produces at its capacity, 4.5 million barrels a day in 2030.
  • 2026. Output in Bahrain, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates is set to the average of the year's months: those published, to July 2026, then the last one held, which is an assumption for 24% of the months. The cut is 6.1 million barrels a day. The other 82 countries with at least 6 published months are set to them in the same way, a net 1.5 more, led by Brazil, the United States, Venezuela, Mexico and Norway.

How the result is checked

In the bridge, fields producing today lose 5.0 million barrels a day, 2.1% a year: 0.2% in the capped members and 3.7% elsewhere. The remainders lose 0.5. Drilling adds a net 1.2 and new additions 5.2, together slightly more than the losses.

No range is estimated for oil. The result is a single path, and the choices that move it furthest are sized, each on its own, by what the 2030 total would gain without them:

  • OPEC+ members at their capacity and not at their cap: 3.5 million barrels a day more.
  • New projects on schedule and at full capacity: 2.2 more.
  • National remainders on their own trend where a rising one is held level: 1.0 more.
  • The 2026 gap to published months carried to 2030: 1.5 more.
  • New projects not yet started a further year late: 1.2 less, the only choice sized that lowers the total.

Against published outlooks

The result was compared with 97 published figures from 11 publishers. The published months of 2026 are not among them, since the model is now set to those. 59 agree within the tolerance set for that kind of comparison, 31 differ moderately and 7 are further off. Where growth can be compared, in 78 cases, the model grows more slowly in 51; the middle gap is 1.0 percentage points a year, and against the world outlook, which is for total liquids, 1.4; on a crude basis it is 0.2. These tests are not fully independent: 33 set the model against a source its own rows are built on, and only 7 of the graded comparisons come from a source it does not use, for Argentina, Brazil, the United Arab Emirates and the United States.

Against the companies' own reports

For 50 companies, the output the model attributes to the company in 2025 is set against the figure in its own annual report. 112 report documents were read; each figure is stored with its page. A company agrees when the model is between 80% and 125% of its figure, judged on its oil and gas figures together; 47 of them report a liquids figure and appear in the chart. 23 companies agree as printed. 10 more agree once both sides count the same thing, for example the same calendar year. 2 differ only in what the report counts. Each has a stated reason, and a company judged on a part of its figure can sit inside the band in the chart while a gap remains on that part; for 8 of them the reason names a cause without yet accounting for the whole gap.

Limits

  • The OPEC+ cap decides the shape of the total. Outlooks that expect OPEC+ to raise output show more oil.
  • Output held level. 15% of 2030 output does not move at all between 2025 and 2030: remainders held level (3.9%), fields still inside their type's peak period (3.0%), fields with no measured decline (2.7%), company-level entries (2.3%) and oil-sands mines (2.0%).
  • The return after the disrupted year is a choice. Output goes back to the fields' path the year after, because no source gives a later figure by country; the one outlook published for the group has it at 98% of its earlier level that year.
  • Company figures rest on what sources print. 7 liquids figures agree only with a remainder slice counted in.

Gas: output, field by field

What each gas field produces today and is likely to produce by 2030, with a field's output held back by the gas it has left.

What the dataset is

The dataset holds one row per field or project per year. 4,048 fields and projects produce in 2025, in 93 countries. It aims to count all gas produced at the well, including gas pumped back into the reservoir or burned off; where a country publishes only the gas it sells, that figure is scaled up. The total is 20% above the latest published world figure (2024) for gas that reaches a market, which is why oil and gas are never added together here.

Output is 5,081 billion cubic metres a year in 2025 and 5,167 in 2030, a change of +1.7%. This is one best estimate, with no price or policy scenarios.

What lies behind the slowly rising total

As for oil, every cubic metre is in one of the 4 layers of the chart, and the fields can be read in 3 ways.

  • With new additions is the total above.
  • As it is now leaves the new additions out; shale drilling and fields held on a company's or country's trend continue at their recent pace. Output falls to 4,900 billion cubic metres a year, a change of −3.6%.
  • No new investment stops all spending at the start of 2026, drilling included. Output falls to 2,895, a change of −43.0%.

Sources

  • The field list is the Global Energy Monitor, Global Oil and Gas Extraction Tracker, March 2026 (CC BY 4.0), the same list as for oil. For many fields it also gives the gas reserves that remain.
  • Measured output and remaining reserves come from regulators that publish them field by field, such as those of Norway, the United Kingdom, Brazil, the American states and the Canadian provinces.
  • National totals come from a country's regulator where it publishes one, and otherwise from international gas statistics, brought to the same basis of all gas produced. That is not reached everywhere: countries with 7% of output, among them Canada and Norway, stay on a narrower or unmeasured basis. A figure that falls below the published figure for gas sold is replaced by that figure scaled up, which governs 363 billion cubic metres.
  • Shale and tight gas uses the drilling reports of the United States Energy Information Administration where a state lies in one basin, and national or regulator trends elsewhere.
  • Who operates and owns each field comes from the same sources as for oil: the field list, regulators' operator and lease records, and company reports and saved pages that name the company for the field.

Where each country's 2025 figure comes from

Of the 2025 total, 26% is measured field by field by a regulator and 33% rests on the field list's own figure. A further 7% comes from company and agency figures. The remaining 34% is not attached to any named field. That is more than twice the share in oil, and it is the main reason the gas result is less certain than the oil one.

The mix differs sharply between countries. In Saudi Arabia, almost none of the output can be tied to a named field.

The following pages follow one field at the other end, Troll in Norway, which produced 41.0 billion cubic metres in 2025. Both its output and the gas it has left are the Norwegian regulator's own figures, which makes it a fully measured field.

From fields to a national total

Each field's 2025 output is taken from the best figure available for it, as for oil: a regulator's measurement replaces the field list's figure, and an older figure is carried forward to 2025.

The national remainder

As for oil, the difference between a country's reported total and the sum of its named fields is kept as one or more extra rows, so that a country matches its reported total. 84 countries carry a remainder. Together the remainders are 1,722 billion cubic metres a year, 34% of the total; named fields and projects account for the other 66%.

A remainder has no reservoir, so no rule about the gas remaining can apply to it. 53% of the remainder volume is held level, because a rising national trend is not carried forward where no named field stands behind it. The rule is one-sided, since a falling trend is carried: it holds 909 billion cubic metres level in 24 countries, and carrying their trends would add 119 by 2030, or 100 leaving out countries whose named fields already carry the rise. 28% follows the country's own measured decline, 14%, in the United States, follows a published national outlook, and the rest takes the shape of the country's named fields. 28% of the remainder has no field type at all.

Field types

Named fields are given one of 7 types, from location, water depth, size, the kind of rock and whether the gas comes out with oil; entries added from company reports, 2% of output, have none.

Shale and tight gas is the largest type, with 18% of output. It is also the type whose output largely does not depend on the gas left in any one field, as the next page explains.

How much is known about the gas remaining

A reserves figure exists for fields that produce 46% of the output of named fields. Where there is none, the rule that gives this chapter its opening line cannot be used, and the field follows the simpler path described on the next page.

Who stands behind the output

On the same rule as for oil, 52% of 2025 output carries ownership shares and a further 14% names the operator alone. Of the 140 rows for new projects and expansions, 85 carry shares.

Looking forward

A producing field's path depends on what is known about it:

  • The gas remaining. 997 fields, 23% of 2025 output. They change by −10.7% by 2030.
  • Drilling. 863 fields, most of them shale and tight gas, 18% of output, hold the net trend of their basin or country over the latest 4 years available. They change by +13.8%.
  • The oil field. 1,369 fields, 8% of output, produce gas with oil and follow the oil of their field, state or country.
  • The type's usual decline. 669 fields without reserves, 5% of output, decline at their type's rate, by 37%.
  • A stated or held path. 143 entries, 11% of output, follow a company's stated trend or are held level.

The rule for the gas remaining

A field holds its output while that takes less than a set share of the gas it has left in a year, a ceiling set by type and size. Beyond that, output falls each year at the decline rate published for fields of its kind, steeper as output falls, never taking more than the ceiling share. Fields already measured past their peak decline regardless.

Troll has 559 billion cubic metres left and a ceiling of 8.0%. It holds its output until the test fails in 2028, then falls by an average of 7.5% a year to 32.4 in 2030.

New additions and one adjusted year

New additions are counted and delayed as for oil: of the 140 rows, 123 carry an addition and reach 266 billion cubic metres a year by 2030, 22% of it in Qatar. A further 54 announced projects have no investment decision and are left out. In 2026, output in Bahrain, Iraq, Kuwait and Qatar is set from the months published, with the last one held to the end of the year, an assumption for 33% of the months; the cut is 134. The other 24 countries with monthly figures are set to their published months against the same months a year earlier, a net 132 more, led by the United States, Russia, Algeria, Nigeria and Canada; this is not carried past the year. In Qatar, export plant reported destroyed keeps 20.3 a year out to 2030.

How the result is checked

No range is estimated for gas. The result is a single path, and the choices that could be sized are given by what the 2030 total would gain without them: remainders on their own trend where a rising one is held level, 119 billion cubic metres a year more; no lasting loss after the disrupted year, 20.3 more; and the 2026 gap to published months carried to 2030, 132 more. Each raises the total, so the path sits at the cautious end of its own choices. The effect of new projects arriving on schedule and at full capacity is not sized.

Against published outlooks

The result was compared with 26 published figures from 7 publishers. The published months of 2026 that the model is now set to are not among them. 19 agree within the tolerance set for that kind of comparison, 6 differ moderately and 1 is further off. A further 2 could not be graded. Published gas figures usually count only gas that reaches a market, so growth is compared and not levels, on the assumption that the gap between gas produced and gas sold stays constant. In the 21 outlook comparisons the model grows more slowly in 14; the middle gap is 0.7 percentage points a year, and against the world outlook 0.4. As for oil, these are not fully independent tests: only 1 of the graded comparisons comes from a source the model does not use.

Against the companies' own reports

The same 50 companies as for oil are set against their own annual reports, on the rule described there: a company agrees when the model is between 80% and 125% of its figure; 44 of them report a gas figure and appear in the chart. For gas the model and the report often count different things, because reports give gas sold while the dataset counts all gas produced. Several steps bring the model to the report's basis before comparing: a company's American wells are credited at its net share of them, taken from its own count of wells; where a regulator measures gas pumped back, the company's share of it is taken out; and liquids the report counts apart are put back as gas. The first is a proxy that reads low for a company with many small interests.

Limits

  • A large remainder. 34% of output is tied to no field: it is held level or follows a national trend, and the rule for it leans low.
  • The disrupted year. Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates publish no monthly gas figures and are not adjusted, and the lasting loss is carried to 2030 on a report that gives no end date. The year after, Qatar returns to its path at once, which overstates it by about 27.5 billion cubic metres.
  • Not one basis everywhere. 360 billion cubic metres are on a narrower or unmeasured basis than all gas produced, which understates those countries against the rest.
  • The gas remaining is known for part of the fields only. The rule built on it governs 23% of output.
  • All gas produced is more than gas sold. Where much of the gas is pumped back into the reservoir, the dataset overstates what reaches a market.
  • Fields with no reserves figure decline fast: by 37%, at a rate for the type.
