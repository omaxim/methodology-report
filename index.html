Auto: plug-in share of new vehicles

How much of each car maker's output will be plug-in vehicles by 2030, built mainly from what the makers themselves publish.

What the dataset is

The dataset holds one row per car maker per year, for 42 vehicle makers worldwide that together reported 90.8 million vehicles in 2025, counting passenger vehicles worldwide. Each row gives the maker's total volume and the share of it that is plug-in: battery electric, plug-in hybrid or fuel cell. Ordinary hybrids, which cannot be charged from the grid, do not count.

Weighted by volume, the plug-in share across these makers rises from 24% in 2025 to 49% in 2030. This is one best estimate. It contains no policy scenarios and no forecast of our own: each maker moves towards the best published evidence of where it is heading.

What it is not

The figure is an average across the makers that could be sourced, not a share of the world market. A maker that publishes nothing usable is absent, and the volumes are those the makers report, on the basis each one chooses: they are not all calendar-year sales, and joint ventures can be counted under both partners.

A maker to follow

This chapter follows Volkswagen Group through each step. It reported 9.0 million vehicles in 2025, of which 16% were plug-in, and the dataset puts it at 50% in 2030. The next page shows where figures like these come from.

Sources

The dataset rests on 1,104 figures taken from 116 documents, published by the makers except where noted: annual reports, quarterly results, sales bulletins and investor presentations. 61 of the figures come from the outside sources below, and some from press reports. The figures also cover brands and truck units beyond the 42 makers. Each figure is stored with the document, the page and the exact words it was read from, so any number in the dataset can be checked against its source. 1,055 of the figures also carry a link to the document online; 278 of those links are reconstructed and not confirmed.

Outside sources are used to set a maker's 2030 share where it has published no target, to set the range of every maker, to scale volumes, and for the starting share of Suzuki, Nissan and Mazda. They are:

  • The International Energy Agency's Global EV Outlook 2025, for the plug-in share of each market under policies already stated, and for the size of the world car market.
  • InfluenceMap, a think tank that publishes a 2030 forecast of the electric share of production for 15 of the makers.

Read directly, or derived

814 figures are read directly from a document. The other 290 are derived from published figures by arithmetic, and each one stores its formula. The derivations are simple. The largest group, 106 figures, only converts units, for example a table printed in thousands. No figure is estimated by us; forecasts by the outside sources are stored as read.

For Volkswagen Group the dataset holds 23 figures, 18 of them read directly. One of the derived ones is its PHEV deliveries, Global, 2024, which the company gives only as a growth rate on the following year, so the figure is computed as 428,000 / (1 + 0.5800).

A check against an independent collection

The same makers were first collected by hand in a spreadsheet. Where both hold the same figure, 448 agree within 0.5%, 3 within 2% and 22 differ; each difference was traced to the document. A further 631 figures in the dataset were never in the spreadsheet. With the figures in place, the next page builds each maker's starting point.

Data methodologyAuto

The starting point: 2025

A maker's plug-in share in 2025 is its plug-in vehicles divided by all its vehicles in that year, both taken from its own reports where it publishes them. Nothing is adjusted: if a maker publishes its plug-in volume for some regions only, the regions it leaves out count as zero. For Suzuki, Nissan and Mazda the share is InfluenceMap's, for an earlier year. 5 makers have no 2025 share in the dataset (Stellantis, Leapmotor, Li Auto, PACCAR and Aston Martin); they are left out of that year's average, and their paths start from an earlier share or from zero.

What counts as volume

Makers do not report on a common basis. 36 of the 42 are recorded as sales, which includes the deliveries of makers that sell direct. The other 6 publish deliveries, wholesale or production instead, and the dataset uses what each one publishes. The largest maker, Toyota, accounts for 12% of the 2025 volume, so no single maker decides the result.

Makers that start at the top

11 makers already sell only plug-in vehicles, 2 of them with no 2025 share recorded. They stay there.

Starting points known to be low

For Stellantis, Ford and GM the published plug-in volume is incomplete, for example covering one region or one business segment, while the total volume is global. Their 2025 shares are therefore too low. Together they make up 15% of volume. Where such a maker has a 2025 value, it pulls the 24% starting point of the whole set down. It does not lower the 2030 result, because their 2030 shares come from forward evidence and not from the starting point. The next page explains that forward evidence.

Looking forward

Each maker's 2030 share comes from the best evidence that exists for it. The kinds of evidence are ranked, and a maker uses the first kind it has:

  • Its own target, or already all plug-in. 24 makers, 57% of 2030 volume: 11 already all plug-in and 13 with a target. 7 of the targets, 34% of volume, are for battery cars alone and are used as stated, with no plug-in hybrids added.
  • A company statement that is not a plug-in target as it stands. 3 makers, 9%: 2 targets that include ordinary hybrids, with the hybrids removed using the maker's current mix, and a stated expectation (Honda).
  • InfluenceMap's forecast for the maker. 6 makers, 22%.
  • The International Energy Agency's outlook for the maker's home market, split with overseas where the maker publishes the split. 8 makers, 11%.
  • None of these: PACCAR, a truck maker the car outlooks do not apply to, is held at zero. 1 maker, 0.2%.

A straight line, then hold

The share moves in a straight line from the 2025 value to the evidence value, passing through any interim target the maker has published, and holds once the target year is reached. No maker is ever projected below its own 2025 share. Nothing is fitted to past growth.

Volkswagen Group is on the strongest kind of evidence. The dataset records its target as: 50% BEV globally by 2030 — NEW AUTO strategy release 13 Jul 2021. It is a target for battery cars alone, and the company has neither restated nor withdrawn it. The dataset therefore takes it from 16% to 50% in a straight line.

Volumes

Every maker's volume grows at the rate of the world car market in the International Energy Agency's stated-policies outlook, +15.9% by 2030. Together the makers reach 105.1 million vehicles in 2030. The combined share in every year weights each maker by this 2030 volume, so the mix of makers is held fixed and the line shows only how their shares change.

How far the result could move

The range is not a statistical interval. It is the same calculation run again with every maker at the lowest, and then the highest, of the evidence values that exist for it: its own target, its converted statement, InfluenceMap's forecast and the International Energy Agency's outlook. That gives 46% to 59% around the 49% result.

No value is taken below a maker's starting share. For 24 makers the evidence values then differ; for the other 18 they do not, and there is no range. Most of the makers with a range, 13, already sit at their lowest value, which is why the range reaches further up than down.

Against an outside view

The International Energy Agency puts the world plug-in share at 42% in 2030 under stated policies. The dataset's 49% is higher, and so is the bottom of its range. The figures are not like for like: the agency describes the whole market under current policy, while the dataset describes what this set of makers say they intend where they say it, and outside forecasts elsewhere, the agency's own among them.

Limits

  • Mixed volume bases. Sales, deliveries, wholesale and production are added together as reported.
  • Joint ventures. Output of Chinese joint ventures can appear under both partners; no single ownership convention is applied.
  • Incomplete starting points. Stellantis, Ford and GM start too low, as described earlier.
  • Targets are intentions. A published target is taken at its word until the maker withdraws it. The maker then moves to the next kind of evidence, which may itself predate the withdrawal.

Air: CO₂ from passenger flights

How much CO₂ each airline's flights emit today and by 2031, estimated for every airline, aircraft and route at the same time.

What the dataset is

The dataset holds one row per aircraft per year: 42,263 aircraft in service in 2026, and 50,609 once those arriving later are counted. Each row gives the airline group that flies the aircraft, its type, the distance it flies, the passengers it carries, the fuel it burns and the CO₂ this produces. These are worked out per airline and aircraft type and shared equally among that airline's aircraft of the type. The aircraft belong to 723 airline groups and fly 48,625 one-way routes, 24,332 airport pairs, between 3,560 airports.

Together they emit 892 million tonnes of CO₂ in 2026 and 1,069 in 2031. Passengers grow from 4.8 to 5.9 billion, and CO₂ per passenger flight falls from 185 to 181 kilograms. This is one best estimate. It contains no policy scenarios.

Why it has to be estimated

No source reports emissions by airline for the whole world. Departures boards say where airlines fly. Registers say which aircraft they own. Some regulators count flights or passengers, and the European trading scheme verifies CO₂ for flights in its area. Each covers a part. The dataset is the single set of values that agrees best with all of them at once.

An airline to follow

This chapter follows Vueling. In 2026 the dataset gives it 147 aircraft of 5 types, 34.3 million passengers and 3.4 million tonnes of CO₂, which is 99 kilograms per passenger flight. The next page shows what such figures rest on.

Data methodologyAir

Sources

The model draws on 24 groups of sources, some of them used only for checking. The main ones are:

  • Routes. Airport departures boards, which we collect daily, give the passenger routes flown in the weeks observed and how often. Those weeks are scaled to a year with the monthly traffic pattern that regulators publish for 880 airports in Europe, the United States and Brazil, and with a regional pattern elsewhere. That scaling is itself adjusted to fit the rest of the evidence.
  • Counted flights. Aviation authorities in the United States, Brazil, Colombia, the United Kingdom and Japan publish departures by airline and route. Many more publish totals by airport.
  • Aircraft tracking. Public records of aircraft positions show which airline flew a route and set a lower limit on each airline's fleet and flying hours.
  • Fleets. National registers, the manufacturers' own counts and their order books.
  • Fuel. Fuel burned per kilometre by aircraft type, from the filings of American airlines and from EUROCONTROL, the European air traffic agency.
  • Verified CO₂. The emissions that airlines report, and auditors verify, under the European trading scheme. The British scheme is used only as a check.
  • Growth. The market forecasts that Airbus, Boeing and COMAC publish.

What the sources pin down

From these sources come 46 kinds of evidence, 156,657 individual figures in all. 84,354 are read from sources; the rest are rules and physical limits. They bear on different parts of the calculation:

Coverage is uneven. Regulators count the flights on 28% of routes. On every other route the number of flights starts from the departures boards and is adjusted to fit the rest of the evidence.

A regulator counts Vueling's flights only on its routes to the United Kingdom. What holds its other figures in place is the departures boards, the tracking of its aircraft, its fleet and the CO₂ it has verified under the European scheme. In the dataset every aircraft type it flies has direct evidence on its fleet or its hours.

Data methodologyAir

Everything is estimated at once

CO₂ is the end of a chain. Flights on a route are shared between the airlines that fly it. Each airline's flights are flown by aircraft of particular types. The types and distances give the hours flown, the fuel burned and the seats offered.

If the links were estimated one after another, evidence used late would overwrite what was settled early, and verified CO₂ could never correct a wrong number of flights. So the model looks for the values of all links together. Each piece of evidence enters once, with its own stated tolerance, and the model finds the values that miss the evidence by the least overall. There are 219,570 unknowns. Where no evidence reaches one, it stays at a plain default, such as the departures boards being right.

How closely the evidence is met

31 kinds of evidence are met within their tolerance and 11 are strained; none is missed outright. These verdicts are for a kind of evidence as a whole; single figures within a kind can miss by much more. The other 4 record that a regulator lists no flights by an airline on a route, and the model sets those to zero. The hardest evidence to satisfy is on verified emissions and on the mix of aircraft types, where what a source covers has to be matched to the model's own routes.

What the 2026 figures rest on

Measured by CO₂, 68% comes from airline fleets with direct evidence on their size or hours, and 20% from flying inferred from similar airlines. The remaining 12% belongs to fleets larger than any source supports, or that registers list but tracking never saw. 41% of the aircraft are placeholders: the model's count for an airline and type exceeds the aircraft the registers name. All CO₂ is assigned to passengers, none to cargo in the hold.

Looking forward

Every year to 2031 is part of the same estimate as 2026. A later year differs from it in these ways.

  • Demand. Each route grows at the rate the manufacturers forecast for the regions it connects. Forecasts exist for 98% of routes; the typical route grows 3.4% a year. Passengers overall grow faster, by 4.1% a year, because routes in fast-growing markets grow much faster than the typical one.
  • Aircraft leaving. Existing aircraft retire according to their type and age. Net of arrivals in the same fleet, 3,477 leave by 2031.
  • Aircraft arriving. The order books set how many new aircraft arrive, as a target the estimate may fall short of. 44% of the deliveries in the period cannot be tied to an airline already flying that type, and the model places them with the airlines that need them. Where an airline still needs more aircraft than the order books can give, the model adds them: 2,219 by 2031, backed neither by an order nor by another airline giving them up.
  • What stays the same. The share of seats filled and the fuel each type burns per kilometre do not change. There is one CO₂ factor per kilogram of fuel in every year, so no cleaner fuels. Emissions per passenger improve only because the mix of aircraft types and routes shifts.

The fleet grows from 42,263 to 47,132 aircraft. CO₂ grows 3.7% a year, more slowly than passengers, and CO₂ per passenger flight changes by −2.0% over the period.

For Vueling the fleet goes from 147 to 173 aircraft and CO₂ from 3.4 to 3.9 million tonnes.

How far the result could move

The model is run 240 times with its fitted values varied within what the evidence allows. The middle of these runs gives the result, and the range between the lowest and the highest tenth is 1,066 to 1,071 million tonnes in 2031. That is 0.5% of the total, and it is too narrow to be read as the real uncertainty: it varies the fitted values only, and takes each route's flights and airline split, the fleet changes after 2026, the order books and the growth forecasts as given. Figures for single aircraft are middle values and do not add up exactly to this total.

For a single aircraft the range, measured a little more widely, is far larger: 27% of its own CO₂ in 2026 and 42% in 2031. The fit itself accounts for most of it. Disagreement between the growth forecasts and the timing of deliveries add little.

A test on airlines kept out

The passengers filed by 15 airlines in 3 regions were kept out of the estimate and compared with it afterwards. A range meant to contain the true figure 80% of the time contains it for 6 of the 15, so the ranges are too narrow, mainly because an airline is not always given all of its own flights. The test was run on the previous model, whose ranges covered only how full the seats are, and has not been repeated on the current one.

A second test removes whole routes. On 21,296 such routes the model's split of flights between airlines is wrong by 25%, against 28% for a simple rule based on airline size. The number of flights on an unseen route is not predicted well: where no count exists, that number rests on the departures boards. This test too was run on a different network from the current one.

Limits

  • Retirements are understated. The model retires a net 695 aircraft a year, by age, so it misses aircraft parked for commercial reasons; the fleet ages more than in reality, which understates the gain in efficiency.
  • Fleets no source supports carry at least 12% of 2026 CO₂, and the 2,219 aircraft the model adds have no order behind them.
  • Verified CO₂ is met only loosely, because a trading scheme's area has to be rebuilt from route endpoints.
  • Seats filled are held constant, since the available series are level or falling a little.

Oil: crude output, field by field

What each oil field produces today and is likely to produce by 2030, built up from individual fields and tied to each country's reported total.

What the dataset is

The dataset holds one row per field or project per year. 13,479 fields and projects produce in 2025, in 88 countries. It covers crude oil and lease condensate. Biofuels and natural gas liquids are outside it, so its totals are lower than figures quoted for all liquids.

Output is 84.5 million barrels a day in 2025 and 84.7 in 2030, a change of +0.2% that leaves the total almost unchanged. This is one best estimate, with no price or policy scenarios.

Why a steady total hides large movements

Every barrel is placed in one of the 4 layers of the chart, so the same fields can be read in 3 ways.

  • With new additions is the total above: everything that produces today plus the projects already decided.
  • As it is now leaves the new additions out. Drilling continues at its recent pace on shale and oil sands plays and in countries, chiefly Russia and China, whose mature fields are kept up by steady drilling. Output then falls to 80.0 million barrels a day, a change of −5.3%.
  • No new investment stops all spending at the start of 2026. Output falls to 55.6, a change of −34.2%.

Data methodologyOil

From fields to a national total

Each field's 2025 output is taken from the best figure available for it. A regulator's measurement replaces the field list's figure for the same barrels. Where the field list's figure is for an earlier year, it is carried forward to 2025 along the path of the field's type, described on the next page.

The national remainder

The named fields of a country rarely add up to the output the country reports. The difference is kept as extra rows for the country, the national remainder, so that every country matches its reported total in 2025. It is a balancing figure, not a field.

73 countries carry a remainder. Together the remainders are 12.3 million barrels a day, 15% of the 2025 total; named fields and projects account for the other 85%. A remainder has no reservoir of its own, so it is given no decline curve. It follows the country's own trend over 2022 to 2025, and a rising trend is held level where new named fields explain the rise.

Field types

Named fields are given one of 7 types, from location, water depth, size and the kind of oil; company-level entries in Russia and China, 6% of output, have none. The type decides how long a field stays at its peak and how fast it declines afterwards, so it matters more for the forward view than for the starting point.

The types differ greatly in number: 7,614 small onshore fields and 4,575 shale oil units, many of them single leases, against only 138 giant onshore fields.

Who stands behind the output

A company is written on a field only where a saved source names it for that field, and a share only where the source prints it. On that rule 66% of 2025 output carries ownership shares and a further 19% names the operator alone. Within that, for 12% of output a regulator's records split the field between its operators by what each produced. The remainders have no field and so no company here. Of the 155 rows for new projects and expansions, 149 name a company and 134 carry shares, which covers 97% of their 2030 output.

What counts as a new addition

New additions are output that did not exist in 2025: projects decided, announced or under construction, expansions of producing fields, and fields still building up to their peak. Only what a project produces above its own 2025 level is an addition, so the additions start at nothing. Outside the OPEC+ members held at quota, 119 fields and projects carry them. 99 had not started in 2025; the other 20 already produced 1.3 million barrels a day between them, which stays with the fields producing today.

Johan Sverdrup is of the type "Offshore shelf". With the starting point of every field fixed, the next page moves it forward.

Looking forward

A producing field follows the path of its type: it holds its peak for as long as fields of that type have done in the past, then declines at the rate published for that type. A long enough regulator series gives a field its own measured decline instead. Shale, tight oil and oil sands do not decline in this way: new wells set their pace, measured over 2022 to 2025 (or a source's latest 4 years) and held to 2030. Oil-sands mines are held at capacity.

Deepwater fields fall furthest, by 21.5%. Johan Sverdrup holds its peak until 2028 and then declines by 9.0% a year, from 685 to 568 thousand barrels a day.

New additions, OPEC+ and one adjusted year

  • New additions reach 4.7 million barrels a day by 2030. A project not yet started begins 1 year later than announced and reaches 80% of its capacity. Brazil gives 29% of them, the 8 largest of 25 countries 83%.
  • OPEC+. Members whose output is set by quota are capped at their output of December 2025; where capacity falls below that, output falls with it. The cap applies to 29% of 2030 output. While it binds, a new project displaces the member's other fields, so 15 projects add nothing.
  • 2026. Output in Bahrain, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates is set to the level of the months published so far, a cut of 2.9 million barrels a day.

Fields producing today lose 5.0 million barrels a day, 2.1% a year, net of capped OPEC+ members stepping up; the remainders lose 0.6. Drilling adds a net 1.1 and new additions 4.7, together slightly more than the losses.

How the result is checked

No range is estimated for oil: the result is a single path, checked in the ways below.

Against published outlooks

The result was compared with 125 published figures from 11 publishers, among them the International Energy Agency, OPEC and the US Energy Information Administration. 29 are statistics already published for 2026; the rest are outlooks. 81 agree within the tolerance set for that kind of comparison, 34 differ moderately and 10 are further off. A further 10 could not be graded, because they fall in the disrupted year or depend on a condition. These are not fully independent tests: the model draws on some of the same publishers, and only 7 of the graded comparisons come from a source it does not use.

Against the companies' own reports

For 50 companies, the output the model attributes to the company in 2025 is set against the figure in its own annual report. 112 report documents were read; each figure is stored with its page. A company agrees when the model is between 80% and 125% of its figure, judged on its oil and gas figures together. 22 companies agree as printed. 10 more agree once both sides count the same thing, for example the same calendar year. 1 differs only in what its report counts. For 17 a gap remains. Each has a stated reason; for 10 of them the reason names a cause without yet accounting for the whole gap.

Limits

  • The OPEC+ cap decides the shape of the total. Outlooks that expect OPEC+ to raise output show more oil.
  • Fields held at their peak. 47 fields, 3.6% of 2030 output, stay level because their type's peak period lasts beyond 2030; 44 more, 2.7%, for lack of a measured decline.
  • Company figures rest on what sources print. 7 liquids figures agree only with a remainder slice counted in.

Gas: output, field by field

What each gas field produces today and is likely to produce by 2030, with a field's output held back by the gas it has left.

What the dataset is

The dataset holds one row per field or project per year. 4,011 fields and projects produce in 2025, in 93 countries. It aims to count all gas produced at the well, including gas pumped back into the reservoir or burned off; where a country publishes only the gas it sells, that figure is scaled up. The total is 18% above the latest published world figure (2024) for gas that reaches a market, which is why oil and gas are never added together here.

Output is 4,962 billion cubic metres a year in 2025 and 5,068 in 2030, a change of +2.1%. This is one best estimate, with no price or policy scenarios.

What lies behind the slowly rising total

As for oil, every cubic metre is in one of the 4 layers of the chart, and the fields can be read in 3 ways.

  • With new additions is the total above.
  • As it is now leaves the new additions out; shale drilling and fields held on a company's or country's trend continue at their recent pace. Output falls to 4,804 billion cubic metres a year, a change of −3.2%.
  • No new investment stops all spending at the start of 2026. Output falls to 2,847, a change of −42.6%.

Looking forward

A producing field's path depends on what is known about it:

  • The gas remaining. 997 fields, 24% of 2025 output. They change by −10.7% by 2030.
  • Drilling. 863 fields, most of them shale and tight gas, 19% of output, hold the net trend of their basin or country over the latest 4 years available. They change by +13.8%.

  • A stated or held path. 143 entries, 11% of output, follow a company's stated trend or are held level.

The rule for the gas remaining

A field holds its output while that takes less than a set share of the gas it has left in a year, a ceiling set by type and size. Beyond that, output falls each year at the decline rate published for fields of its kind, steeper as output falls, never taking more than the ceiling share. Fields already measured past their peak decline regardless.

Troll has 559 billion cubic metres left and a ceiling of 8.0%. It holds its output until the test fails in 2028, then falls by an average of 7.5% a year to 32.4 in 2030.

New additions and one adjusted year

New additions are counted and delayed as for oil: 93 fields and phases reach 265 billion cubic metres a year by 2030, 22% of it in Qatar. A further 54 announced projects have no investment decision and are left out. In 2026, output in Bahrain, Kuwait and Qatar is set to the months published so far, a net cut of 136.

How the result is checked

No range is estimated for gas, so the result is a single path, checked in the same ways as oil.

Against published outlooks

The result was compared with 40 published figures from 7 publishers; 15 are statistics already published for 2026 and the rest outlooks. 29 agree within the tolerance set for that kind of comparison, 10 differ moderately and 1 is further off. A further 1 could not be graded. Published gas figures usually count only gas that reaches a market, so growth is compared and not levels, on the assumption that the gap between gas produced and gas sold stays constant. As for oil, these are not fully independent tests: only 1 of the graded comparisons comes from a source the model does not use.

Against the companies' own reports

The same 50 companies as for oil are set against their own annual reports, on the rule described there: a company agrees when the model is between 80% and 125% of its figure. For gas the model and the report often count different things, because reports give gas sold while the dataset counts all gas produced. Where a regulator measures the gas pumped back, it is taken out before comparing.

Limits

  • A large remainder. 32% of output is tied to no field: it is held level or follows a national trend.
  • The gas remaining is known for part of the fields only. The rule built on it governs 24% of output.
  • All gas produced is more than gas sold. Where much of the gas is pumped back into the reservoir, the dataset overstates what reaches a market.
  • Fields with no reserves figure decline fast: by 37%, at a rate for the type.
